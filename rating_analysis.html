Verdict. No — the raters agree on only 2 of 8 metrics (Content Accuracy and Visual Quality, where all three land within one point on every video). The other six do not hold. But the failure is structured, not random: about half the total variance is genuine signal about the videos, a fifth is pure rater-severity offset, and a third is residual disagreement. That means the instrument is fixable — the fixes are rubric anchoring, a mandatory "no basis to judge" option, and rater calibration, not simply "recruit more raters."

Caveat that governs everything below. Three videos per rater. Every per-metric percentage is out of 3, and the reliability coefficients carry very wide confidence intervals. Treat this as a pilot that tells you which metrics to fix, not one that ranks systems.

Severity-centering each rater's scores raises α from 0.24 → 0.42, and the consistency-form ICC(3,1) is 0.41 versus the absolute-form 0.31. In other words: the raters are ordering the videos more similarly than their raw numbers suggest — they are simply anchored to different points on the scale. That is exactly what an anchored rubric plus a calibration round fixes.

4Are the metrics doing their job?

A metric earns its place if it (a) separates videos, (b) uses the scale, and (c) is not a duplicate of another metric. Several fail all three.

4.1 Discrimination — does the metric separate the videos at all?

Logic is the clearest failure. qing scored it 5/5/5 and vshourie 3/3/3 — both raters gave a flat, video-independent score, with opposite anchors. A metric with zero between-video variance carries no information about the videos, only about who rated them.

4.2 Scale use — ceiling effects

Three metrics (Logic, Bloom, ICAP) are majority-5. A metric that is mostly 5 cannot show improvement in a system you are trying to improve — you have no headroom to measure into. Note also that no rater ever used score 1 among the three usable raters; the effective scale is 2–5, i.e. four points, not five.

4.3 Redundancy — are eight metrics measuring eight things?

Two clusters emerge, plus one outlier:

  • Pedagogical cluster — Objective Coverage, Learning Adaptation, Bloom Alignment (r = 0.85–0.90 with each other). Raters appear to be forming a single "is this pedagogically sound" impression and writing it into three boxes.
  • Presentation cluster — Content Accuracy, Visual Quality, ICAP Alignment (r = 0.78–0.85). ICAP landing here rather than with Bloom is a red flag: ICAP is an engagement construct, but it is tracking visual polish.
  • Logic — correlates negatively with Content Accuracy (−0.45) and Visual Quality (−0.54). A near-zero-variance metric will produce unstable correlations, so do not over-read the sign; the message is that Logic is not behaving like the others.

Practically: eight metrics are probably measuring three to four constructs. That is not necessarily wrong — but it means the eight scores should not be treated as independent evidence, and an average across all eight over-weights whichever construct is represented three times.

5Root causes, from the comments

The free-text comments explain the numbers. Five distinct failure modes, each with a specific fix.

Cause 1 — A default score was submitted as if it were a judgment

qing_yue_chen, V2 · Bloom Alignment · score 5 — "no input"
qing_yue_chen, V2 · ICAP Alignment · score 5 — "no input"
qing_yue_chen, V3 · Bloom Alignment · score 5 — "no input"
qing_yue_chen, V3 · ICAP Alignment · score 5 — "no input"

Four cells where the rater explicitly wrote that they had nothing to say, yet a 5 was recorded. These four are not ratings — they are missing data recorded as the maximum score, and they sit precisely on top of the two worst-disagreement metrics. Dropping just these four cells lifts α from 0.24 → 0.38 and ICC(2,1) from 0.31 → 0.42, on 20 remaining cells.

Fix: no pre-filled default; an explicit, first-class "Cannot judge / Not applicable" option that is stored as missing rather than as a number; and no submission until every item is either scored or marked N/A.

Cause 2 — Construct confusion: the wrong judgment under the metric's name

qing_yue_chen, V1 · Visual Quality — "no obvious hallucinations at all. But the transition… is a bit stiff"
qing_yue_chen, V2 · Content Accuracy — "the animation isn't vivid"

"No hallucinations" is an accuracy judgment written under Visual Quality; "the animation isn't vivid" is a visual judgment written under Content Accuracy. The raters are not distinguishing the constructs the metric names claim to separate — which is precisely what the 0.85 correlation between those two metrics in §4.3 shows quantitatively.

Fix: every metric needs a one-line inclusion and exclusion definition — "Content Accuracy covers factual and conceptual correctness of the narration and on-screen text. It does not cover how appealing or clear the animation is; rate that under Visual Quality."

Cause 3 — One metric name, two constructs

qing_yue_chen, V2 · Logic · 5 — "overall the logic doesn't have mistakes"
vshourie, V1 · Logic · 3 — "Jumps between most slides feel abrupt, without slowly building into the content overtime"
vshourie, V2 · Logic · 3 — "Content could flow smoother for a better lesson overall"

qing reads "Logic" as absence of logical errors — effectively a binary check that always passes, hence 5/5/5. vshourie reads it as quality of narrative sequencing and transitions — a graded property, hence 3/3/3. They are not disagreeing about the video; they are rating two different things.

Fix: split into Logical Correctness (binary or 3-point: are there reasoning errors?) and Instructional Sequencing (graded: does the material build coherently?). Expect this one change to fix the worst-behaved metric in the set.

Cause 4 — The reference standard is left to the rater

vshourie, V3 · Objective Coverage · 2 — "Learning goals partially covered, only 2/4 covered as per my interpretation"
huaiyuan_real, V3 · Objective Coverage · 3 — "even it's introductory, some real code should be included"

Objective Coverage is being scored against each rater's private reconstruction of what the learning objectives were, and against each rater's private standard for depth. vshourie is counting objectives (2 of 4); huaiyuan is judging pedagogical sufficiency. Without a stated objective list, this metric cannot converge.

Fix: show the raters the video's declared learning objectives alongside it, and make the metric mechanical — "how many of the N stated objectives are substantively addressed?" This converts a judgment call into a count, which is where inter-rater agreement is cheapest to buy.

Cause 5 — Framework metrics without the framework

huaiyuan_real, V2 · Bloom · 5 — "I think just understanding is fine."
vshourie, V2 · Bloom · 3 — "Even if not presented, this is not likely to be in alignment with Bloom (active)"
huaiyuan_real, V2 · ICAP · 3 — "It didn't introduce any active parts."
vshourie, V2 · ICAP · 3 — "Barely engages the student at all"

Bloom and ICAP are ordinal taxonomies — Remember/Understand/Apply/Analyze/Evaluate/Create, and Passive/Active/Constructive/Interactive. Here they have been flattened onto a generic 1–5 "goodness" scale, and the raters then disagree about whether reaching a lower tier is a defect. huaiyuan says "just understanding is fine" and scores 5; vshourie treats not-active as a deficit and scores 3. Both are internally consistent. The scale is what is broken.

Fix: rate these on their native categorical scale. Ask "what is the highest ICAP mode this video elicits: Passive / Active / Constructive / Interactive?" and "what is the highest Bloom level targeted?" Score agreement with a nominal/ordinal κ. Whether a given level is good is a separate question the researcher answers against the video's stated intent — never the rater's private standard.

7Instrument & protocol fixes

Ordered by expected reliability gain per unit of effort. The first three cost almost nothing and address the largest measured effects.

P0 Remove the default score; add an explicit "Cannot judge"

Directly measured: 4 non-response cells recorded as 5. Removing them alone takes α 0.24 → 0.38 and ICC 0.31 → 0.42. Store N/A as missing, never as a number, and block submission on unanswered items. Also reject submissions where every item equals the initial value (the aarsh case).

P0 Write behaviourally anchored descriptors for all 5 scale points

Rater severity is 19.9% of total variance; centering it recovers α 0.24 → 0.42. Anchors are what make "4" mean the same thing to two people. Each metric needs one concrete sentence per level, describing observable video properties — not "good / fair / poor." Note that no rater used score 1 and only Visual Quality dipped to 2: the anchors must actively describe what a 1 and a 2 look like, or the scale stays compressed to 3–5.

P0 Add inclusion/exclusion boundaries between adjacent metrics

Comments show accuracy judgments filed under Visual Quality and vice versa (§5 cause 2), matching the r = 0.85 between them. One line each: what this metric covers, and explicitly which neighbouring metric handles the thing it does not.

P1 Run a calibration round before the real ratings

All raters score the same 2–3 anchor videos, then meet to reconcile every cell differing by ≥2 and record the resolution as an anchor example. This is the standard remedy for the severity spread seen here, and it converts the pilot's disagreements into rubric documentation instead of discarding them.

P1 Re-scale Bloom and ICAP to their native taxonomies

They are ordinal categories, not 1–5 quality scores (§5 cause 5). This also removes their ceiling effect: 67% and 56% of ratings currently sit at 5, leaving no headroom to detect improvement.

P1 Show raters the video's declared learning objectives

Turns Objective Coverage from a private reconstruction into a count against a fixed list (§5 cause 4) — the cheapest agreement gain available in the set.

P2 Collapse eight metrics to the constructs actually being measured

The correlation structure suggests 3–4 constructs (§4.3). Either reduce the instrument, or keep eight but report them grouped — and stop averaging all eight into one number, since that silently triple-weights the pedagogical construct.

P2 Add pairwise preference judgments alongside the ratings

All three raters already agree V1 is best — the coarse signal is real. "Which of these two videos is better on X, and why?" is far more reliable than absolute scoring, needs no anchoring, and gives you a system-comparison result even while the absolute rubric is still being fixed.

P2 Require a comment on any score below 3 or at 5

Comment coverage was 46–83% among usable raters and 4% for the ceiling rater. Extreme scores are where the evidence lives, and mandatory justification is a well-established brake on careless extremes.

P2 Randomize video order per rater and record time-on-task

All three usable raters rated in the fixed order V1, V2, V3, so order and fatigue effects are perfectly confounded with the video. Time-on-task additionally gives you an objective screen for low-effort submissions.

Read this as a budget argument. Reaching a defensible 0.80 by brute force costs 10 raters × every video. The P0 fixes in §7 cost one afternoon of rubric writing and drop that to 6 — and the estimate of 0.417 comes only from removing four bad cells, before any anchoring or calibration. Anchoring and a calibration round would plausibly push single-rater ICC higher still.

Note also the 3-video ceiling on precision: with n = 3 items, the confidence interval on any of these coefficients spans most of the plausible range. The next round needs more videos more than it needs more raters — see §9.

9Proposed next experiment

Design

  • Videos: 12–20, spanning the quality range you actually care about (include known-bad and known-good exemplars — the pilot's three all scored in a narrow band, which is part of why the metrics look non-discriminating).
  • Raters: 5–6 per video, fully crossed on at least a 6-video overlap block so a proper ICC can be computed. Screen out non-responders with the time-on-task and default-value checks.
  • Instrument: the revised metric set from §6 — 2 kept, 3 fixed, 1 split, 2 re-scaled to categorical — with anchored descriptors and a mandatory N/A option.
  • Protocol: calibration round on 2 anchor videos and a reconciliation meeting first; then randomized video order; comments mandatory on extreme scores.
  • Secondary task: pairwise preference on a subset, as an anchoring-free cross-check on the absolute scores.

Pre-registered success criteria

  • Krippendorff's α ≥ 0.667 per metric (retire or redesign any metric that misses it twice).
  • ICC(2,k) ≥ 0.80 for the reported panel mean.
  • No metric with >40% of ratings at a single scale point.
  • No pair of metrics with severity-adjusted r > 0.80 — else merge them.

What you can already claim from this pilot

  • The three usable raters consistently rank V1 highest; two of three produce the identical full ordering.
  • Content Accuracy and Visual Quality are usable metrics today — all three raters agree within one point on every video.
  • Roughly half the score variance is real signal about the videos, so the construct is measurable; the instrument, not the task, is the bottleneck.

What this pilot cannot support

  • Any absolute claim of the form "our system scores X on metric Y."
  • Any comparison between systems on Logic, Bloom, ICAP, or Learning Adaptation.
  • Any average across all eight metrics, given the redundancy structure in §4.3.

Methods

Statistics used, and their caveats
  • Krippendorff's α (interval difference function) — chance-corrected agreement, handles >2 raters. Conventional bands: ≥0.80 good, ≥0.667 tentative, below that unacceptable. Computed over 24 units × 3 raters.
  • ICC(2,1) / ICC(2,k) — two-way random effects, absolute agreement, single measure and k-measure forms. Penalizes systematic rater severity. Bands (Koo & Li): <0.50 poor, 0.50–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good.
  • ICC(3,1) — two-way mixed, consistency. Ignores severity offsets; the gap between it and ICC(2,1) is the size of the severity problem.
  • Spearman–Brown — projects the reliability of a k-rater mean from the single-rater ICC. Assumes exchangeable raters; the projection is optimistic if the added raters resemble aarsh or hua_wei.
  • Variance decomposition — two-way ANOVA sums of squares (item, rater, residual) as a share of total, no interaction term estimable with one observation per cell.
  • Inter-metric correlations — Pearson r on rater-mean-centered scores, so a lenient rater's across-the-board high scores do not inflate them. n = 9 per pair; these are directional evidence only.

Principal limitation: 3 videos. Every coefficient here has a confidence interval wide enough to include both "somewhat worse" and "somewhat better" than reported. The qualitative findings — the aarsh degenerate rows, the "no input" cells scored 5, the two-constructs-under-Logic split, the Bloom/ICAP scale mismatch — do not depend on sample size and are the more actionable half of this report.
